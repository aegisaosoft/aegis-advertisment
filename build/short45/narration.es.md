# Tres y Quince — Spanish narration (US / Latin American)

The Spanish track of the 55-second cut. Not a dubbing of the English: written to
the same beats, in the Spanish a rental fleet owner in the United States actually
speaks.

**Dialect: US / Latin American, deliberately not peninsular.** The audience is the
US Latino market, so: `carro` (not `coche`), `renta` (not `alquiler`), `manejar`
(not `conducir`), `costo` (not `coste`), `gasolina` (not `combustible`). The word
for the gantry is dropped altogether — `pórtico` reads as Spain, `caseta` is a
manned booth and ours are not — and "en ese peaje" carries it without naming it.
`usted` throughout: this is a business owner being addressed about their money.

Note this differs from the two-minute Spanish film, which is peninsular (`coche`,
`pórtico`). If both are to sound like one company, that film needs the same pass.

**The figures are the Spanish screen's own**, because the Spanish cut shows the
Spanish capture: `Total: $100.89`, 15 crossings. The English cut says four hundred
twelve because that is what the English capture says. A film must never narrate a
number the screen behind it contradicts.

**The echo therefore lands on "quince"**, not on the money: the crossing happens at
"las tres y quince" and the bill turns out to be "quince cruces". English gets the
richer version of the same trick — "four-twelve" returning as "four hundred twelve" —
because its screen happens to allow it.

**The voice must be Latin American** — es-MX or es-US from the ElevenLabs library,
never es-ES. A Castilian accent selling to a Miami or Houston fleet owner is heard
in the first two seconds, and it is heard as "this was not made for me".

Runtime ~55s. 13 lines, read at roughly 2.6 words per second.

## Escena 1 — Martes, 3:15 (13.5s)

**0-0** _(max 5.6s)_

> Martes. Las tres y quince de la tarde. Uno de sus carros cruza un peaje.

**0-1** _(max 2s)_

> Nadie lo paga. Todavía no.

**0-2** _(max 3.6s)_

> La factura llega tres semanas después. Con su nombre.
